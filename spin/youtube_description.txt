You're standing on the summit of Chimborazo in Ecuador at dawn, the point on Earth farthest from its centre, when the planet starts to spin faster. Every day is shorter than the last. The Sun races across the sky, the stars streak into arcs, and the oceans pour toward the equator until the Andes become islands. The rock gives way, volcanoes erupt, and you get lighter and lighter, until at 24,000 km/h the ground moves at orbital speed and you float off the top of the world. Earth can't spin any faster, so it throws away its equator, and grows a ring. And from that ring, something else is born.

What is real: the physics behind every step. Chimborazo's summit is 6,384 km from Earth's centre, about 2 km farther out than Everest, because Earth bulges at the equator, and the ground there carries you east at about 1,675 km/h. A faster spin flings water toward the equator: one hour off the day would already raise the sea there by roughly 200 m, and the oceans near the poles would drain. Rock behaves like a very thick fluid under enough stress, so a fast-spinning Earth slowly reshapes itself around its spin. Days on Uranus, Neptune, Saturn and Jupiter last about 17, 16, 10.7 and 9.9 hours, and the dwarf planet Haumea, stretched into an egg by its spin, turns in 3.9 hours. You float off when the speed of the ground equals the speed of an orbit at that height. A ring forms inside a planet's Roche limit, about 19,000 km from Earth's centre for rock, where tides tear clumps apart. Outside it, debris sticks together into moonlets and then a moon. That is the leading explanation for our own Moon: about 4.5 billion years ago a Mars-sized body called Theia hit the young Earth, the debris formed a disk, and the Moon grew from it, possibly in less than a century. Moon rocks are almost identical to Earth's mantle, and back then a day on Earth lasted about 5 hours. The Moon's tides have been braking Earth's spin ever since. Today the Moon moves 3.8 cm farther away every year, and each century the day grows about 1.8 milliseconds longer.

What is not real, or not known: nothing can actually spin up a planet like this. In this thought experiment the air speeds up with the ground; otherwise the winds would be stronger than any hurricane. The sea levels, the 2.2-hour shortest day, the size of the equatorial bulge, when the rock gives way and your weight at each moment are rough estimates from simple models: nobody knows exactly how a real Earth would deform. The volcanoes that erupt are real, but their timing is invented. The way you are carried outward through the ring, the size of the new moon and how fast it forms are simplified, and time-lapse speeds are chosen so the story fits in 16 minutes. Nobody could survive the trip; your suit is part of the thought experiment.

The day you just lost was a few hours long. The Moon spent four and a half billion years stretching it to 24 hours, and it is still at it: every tide you see is the Moon slowing the Earth down.
