You're standing on the summit of Chimborazo in Ecuador at dawn, the point on Earth farthest from its centre, when the planet starts to spin faster. Every day is shorter than the last. The Sun races across the sky, the stars streak into arcs, and the oceans pour toward the equator until the Andes become islands. The rock gives way, volcanoes erupt, and you get lighter and lighter, until at 24,000 km/h the ground moves at orbital speed and you float off the top of the world. Earth can't spin any faster, so it throws away its equator and grows a ring. And out past the edge of that ring, the debris starts to clump together into something new.

What is real: the physics behind every step. Chimborazo's summit is 6,384 km from Earth's centre, about 2 km farther out than Everest, because Earth bulges at the equator, and the ground there carries you east at about 1,675 km/h. A faster spin flings water toward the equator: one hour off the day would already raise the sea there by roughly 200 m, and the oceans near the poles would drain. Under enough stress rock flows like a very thick fluid, so a fast-spinning Earth slowly reshapes itself around its spin. Days on Uranus, Neptune, Saturn and Jupiter last about 17, 16, 10.7 and 9.9 hours, and the dwarf planet Haumea, stretched into an egg by its spin, turns in 3.9 hours. You float off when the speed of the ground equals the speed of an orbit at that height. Rings form inside a planet's Roche limit, about 19,000 km from Earth's centre for rock, where tides tear clumps apart; that is why Saturn's rings never became moons. Outside the limit, debris sticks together into moonlets, and the biggest one sweeps up the rest.

What is not real, or not known: nothing can actually spin up a planet like this. In this thought experiment the air speeds up with the ground; otherwise the winds would be stronger than any hurricane. The sea levels, the 2.2-hour shortest day, the size of the equatorial bulge, when the rock gives way and your weight at each moment are rough estimates from simple models: nobody knows exactly how a real Earth would deform. The volcanoes that erupt are real, but their timing is invented. The way you are carried outward through the ring and how fast the moonlets grow are simplified, and the time-lapse speeds are chosen so the story fits in 12 minutes. Nobody could survive the trip; your suit is part of the thought experiment.

Remember the 5-hour mark on the dial? About 4.5 billion years ago a Mars-sized world hit the young Earth, the debris formed a ring, and our Moon grew out of it, just like the moonlets you watched merge. Back then, a day on Earth lasted about 5 hours. The Moon's tides have been slowing Earth down ever since: today the Moon moves 3.8 cm farther away every year, and each century the day grows about 1.8 milliseconds longer.
